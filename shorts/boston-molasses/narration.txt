In 1919, Boston was hit by a wave of molasses moving at thirty-five miles an hour.

Yes. Molasses. The stuff that barely moves when you pour it.

Beside a busy neighborhood stood a giant steel tank, fifty feet tall, filled with more than two million gallons of the sticky syrup.

There was just one problem. The tank was leaking.

Instead of fixing it, the company painted it brown to hide the drips.

Then, one January afternoon, the steel suddenly split apart.

A fifteen-foot wall of molasses thundered through the streets, crushing buildings and knocking a firehouse off its foundation.

And here's the terrifying part. Molasses moves slowly from a spoon. But millions of gallons released together can behave like a powerful flood.

Once it stopped, the thick, sticky liquid made rescue incredibly difficult.

Twenty-one people died. Around one hundred and fifty were injured.

Years later, the company was found responsible for the tank's dangerously weak construction.

The strangest flood in Boston's history was also one that could have been prevented.
